The brevity of Amara being sometimes reminiscent of
Panini’s sitra-s, Stirin fulfils the role of the Bhasyakara. The
words halifa and sairika are defined by Amara as

खनति तेन तद्रोढास्येदं हा्किैर्कि | (p. 591)

एप brings the above aphoristical statement within the
realm of intelligibility by saying:

goa खनतीति हालिकः | हरं हीति ater: | ese संबन्धी हारिकः |
ada खनतः, हकं वहतः, हसंबन्धिनो वा adage नामनी । (p. 592)

i.e. oxen utilized for ploughing or drawing carts. Similar is
the explanation of the word sairika.

Some words in Amara’s text are desya, local words of
popular origin; for even such a conservative and classical
language as Sanskrit is not completely immune to the influence
of the regional languages. Siirin points out that some words,
more especially names of plants in the Vanausadhivarga, are
of Prakrit origin. The word phamijjaka (citron tree), Sarin
says, has been adopted from the vernacular, phanyjakasabdo
defyah (p. 262). The milk-hedge plant is variously named
Sthunda, simhunda and sihunda. Dhanvantari and Svamin
recognize the Prakrit form sthunda; but Strin gives simhunda
only and explains that it is only irregularly formed with the
loss of ta in simhaiunda (resembling the mouth of a lion).

सिंहतुण्डाभशाखाव्वात्‌ सिंहुण्डः | तकाररोषः | (p. 277)

The word indindira (bee) is given by Strin as an additional
variant of a doubtful character (p. 351); Svamin considers it
a desya word, but Siirin has no comment to offer.
